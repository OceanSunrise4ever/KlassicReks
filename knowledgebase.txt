
Crime and Punishment is a psychological novel and philosophical drama written by Fyodor Dostoevsky and published in 1866. Crime and Punishment follows Rodion Raskolnikov, a poor former student in St. Petersburg who convinces himself that certain extraordinary people have the right to break moral laws for a greater purpose. After committing a murder, he becomes increasingly consumed by guilt, fear, and psychological torment as an investigator closes in on him. The novel explores guilt, morality, justice, redemption, and poverty. It examines the conflict between intellectual theories and moral conscience while suggesting that genuine redemption requires accepting responsibility and reconnecting with others. The main characters include Rodion Raskolnikov, the troubled protagonist; Sofya (Sonya) Marmeladova, a compassionate young woman who encourages him to seek redemption; and Porfiry Petrovich, the clever investigator who gradually uncovers Raskolnikov's guilt. The book is notable for its psychological depth, philosophical themes, exploration of morality and guilt, and influential portrayal of the inner struggles of a person facing the consequences of their actions.

The Idiot is a psychological novel and philosophical drama written by Fyodor Dostoevsky and published in 1869. The Idiot follows Prince Lev Myshkin, a kind-hearted and compassionate young man who returns to Russia after years of medical treatment in Switzerland and becomes involved with members of wealthy society. His innocence and sincerity contrast sharply with the pride, jealousy, and selfishness of those around him, particularly as he becomes caught in a complicated relationship with Nastasya Filippovna and Aglaya Yepanchina. The novel explores innocence, morality, love, society, and human nature. It examines whether genuine goodness and compassion can survive in a society shaped by pride, greed, obsession, and social expectations. The main characters include Prince Myshkin, the compassionate protagonist; Nastasya Filippovna, a deeply troubled woman whose life becomes intertwined with Myshkin's; and Aglaya Yepanchina, a young woman who develops feelings for Myshkin. The book is notable for its psychological depth, philosophical questions about morality and goodness, complex characters, and exploration of the contrast between innocence and corruption.

Demons is a psychological and philosophical novel written by Fyodor Dostoevsky and published in 1872. Demons follows Nikolai Stavrogin and a group of politically radical young people whose ideas and ambitions contribute to growing violence and disorder in a provincial Russian town. As their revolutionary plans become increasingly extreme, the characters struggle with questions of morality, ideology, faith, and personal responsibility. The novel explores political radicalism, nihilism, morality, religion, and social unrest. It examines how extreme ideologies can influence individuals and destabilize society when people abandon personal responsibility and moral boundaries. The main characters include Nikolai Stavrogin, a mysterious and influential figure; Pyotr Stepanovich Verkhovensky, a manipulative revolutionary who organizes the radical group; and Stepan Trofimovich Verkhovensky, an intellectual whose ideas and relationship with his son reflect the generation gap within Russian society. The book is notable for its political commentary, psychological complexity, philosophical themes, and critique of revolutionary extremism and nihilism in 19th-century Russia.

The Brothers Karamazov is a philosophical novel and psychological drama written by Fyodor Dostoevsky and published in 1880. The Brothers Karamazov follows the three Karamazov brothers—Dmitri, Ivan, and Alexei (Alyosha)—as they struggle with their complicated relationships with their father, Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov, and become entangled in a family conflict surrounding his murder. As the brothers respond differently to questions of faith, morality, and responsibility, their relationships are tested by jealousy, resentment, and guilt. The novel explores faith, morality, free will, family, and guilt. It examines the conflict between belief and doubt and questions how much responsibility individuals have for their own actions and the actions of others. The main characters include Dmitri Karamazov, the passionate and impulsive eldest brother; Ivan Karamazov, the intellectual and skeptical middle brother; and Alexei Karamazov, the compassionate youngest brother whose religious faith guides him. The book is notable for its philosophical depth, psychological complexity, exploration of religion and morality, and portrayal of the complicated bonds between family members.

Eugene Onegin is a novel in verse and romantic drama written by Alexander Pushkin and published in 1833. Eugene Onegin follows Eugene Onegin, a wealthy and sophisticated young man who becomes bored with his privileged life and moves to the countryside, where he meets Tatyana Larina, a young woman who falls deeply in love with him. After rejecting Tatyana, Onegin later realizes his feelings for her, but by then their circumstances have changed and she refuses to abandon her marriage. The novel explores love, regret, social class, identity, and fate. It examines how pride, missed opportunities, and social expectations can prevent people from finding happiness and how people may only recognize the value of something after losing it. The main characters include Eugene Onegin, the detached and disillusioned protagonist; Tatyana Larina, the sincere young woman who falls in love with him; and Vladimir Lensky, Onegin's romantic and idealistic friend. The book is notable for its innovative verse form, vivid portrayal of 19th-century Russian society, psychological characterization, and influential role in Russian literature.

The Bronze Horseman is a narrative poem and historical drama written by Alexander Pushkin and published in 1837. The Bronze Horseman follows Yevgeny, a poor and ordinary clerk living in St. Petersburg, whose hopes for a quiet life are destroyed when a devastating flood strikes the city and takes away the woman he loves. As Yevgeny loses his sense of stability and sanity, he becomes obsessed with the statue of Peter the Great, known as the Bronze Horseman. The poem explores nature, fate, power, individual suffering, and social inequality. It examines the conflict between the ambitions of powerful leaders and the lives of ordinary people, questioning the human cost of political progress and grand visions. The main characters include Yevgeny, the tragic protagonist; Parasha, the woman he loves; and Peter the Great, represented by the Bronze Horseman statue and symbolizing the power of the state. The book is notable for its powerful imagery, historical setting, blend of personal tragedy with political themes, and influential place in Russian literature.

Boris Godunov is a historical tragedy and verse drama written by Alexander Pushkin and published in 1831. The play explores power, ambition, guilt, legitimacy, and political conflict. It examines the relationship between rulers and the people while questioning whether political authority can be maintained through ambition and manipulation. The main characters include Boris Godunov, the conflicted tsar; Grigory Otrepyev, the pretender who challenges Boris's rule; and Pimen, an elderly monk and historian who preserves the story of Russia's past. The book is notable for its historical setting, dramatic portrayal of Russian politics, innovative structure, and exploration of the relationship between political power and public opinion.

It examines how the desire for wealth and certainty can consume a person and how attempts to control fate may ultimately lead to one's downfall. The Death of Ivan Ilyich is a novella and philosophical drama written by Leo Tolstoy and published in 1886. As his condition worsens, Ivan reflects on his choices, relationships, and the meaning of his life, eventually questioning whether he has lived authentically. The novella explores death, mortality, meaning, isolation, and authenticity. It examines how fear of death can lead people to reevaluate their priorities and questions whether social status and material success can provide genuine happiness or fulfillment. The main characters include Ivan Ilyich, the dying protagonist; Praskovya Fedorovna, his wife, whose relationship with him becomes increasingly strained; and Gerasim, a compassionate servant who provides Ivan with genuine kindness and comfort. The main characters include Dmitri Nekhlyudov, the nobleman seeking redemption; Katyusha Maslova, a woman whose difficult life is shaped by poverty and social injustice; and Selenin, a government official who represents the bureaucratic system Nekhlyudov comes to question. The book is notable for its strong criticism of Russian social and legal institutions, moral and religious themes, and realistic portrayal of poverty, inequality, and the possibility of personal transformation.

And Then There Were None is a mystery novel and psychological thriller written by Agatha Christie and published in 1939. And Then There Were None follows ten strangers who are invited to an isolated island by a mysterious host, only to discover that each of them has been accused of causing someone's death. As the guests begin dying one by one, the remaining characters become increasingly suspicious and desperate to discover the identity of the killer. The novel explores guilt, justice, fear, isolation, and deception. It examines whether people can escape the consequences of their actions and how fear and suspicion can destroy trust between individuals. The main characters include Justice Wargrave, a retired judge; Vera Claythorne, a young secretary and former governess; and Philip Lombard, a former soldier and adventurer, all of whom become trapped on the island with the other guests. The book is notable for its ingenious mystery structure, suspenseful atmosphere, isolated setting, and innovative use of the classic locked-room mystery format.

Murder on the Orient Express is a mystery novel and detective thriller written by Agatha Christie and published in 1934. The novel explores justice, revenge, morality, deception, and guilt. The main characters include Hercule Poirot, the brilliant detective investigating the murder; Samuel Ratchett, the victim whose hidden past drives the mystery; and Bouc, Poirot's friend and a director of the train company who assists with the investigation. Sense and Sensibility is a classic romance novel of manners written by Jane Austen and published in 1811. The masterpiece follows the impoverished sisters Elinor and Marianne Dashwood as they navigate the rigid social expectations, financial instability, and romantic disappointments of Regency England. After their father's death leaves them near-penniless, the restrained and practical Elinor suffers in silence over her love for the unavailable Edward Ferrars, while the deeply passionate and romantic Marianne openly falls apart after being brutally abandoned by the dashing John Willoughby. The novel deeply explores reason versus emotion, the economic vulnerability of women, societal decorum, and sisterly devotion. Ultimately, it delivers the message that a fulfilling life requires a harmonious balance between cautious judgment ("sense") and emotional openness ("sensibility"). The main characters include Elinor Dashwood, the epitome of emotional restraint; Marianne Dashwood, the impulsive romantic; and Colonel Brandon, a honorable, quiet gentleman who patiently loves Marianne from afar. The work is globally notable for being Jane Austen's first published novel and establishing her signature use of sharp irony, social satire, and profound psychological realism.

Pride and Prejudice is a classic romantic comedy of manners written by Jane Austen and published in 1813. The masterpiece follows the witty and independent Elizabeth Bennet as she navigates the turbulent waters of societal expectations, marital pressure, and financial insecurity in Regency England. After a rocky introduction to the wealthy, aloof aristocrat Mr. Fitzwilliam Darcy, Elizabeth must overcome her initial misjudgments and family scandals—such as her sister Lydia's reckless elopement—while Darcy must conquer his class snobbery to win her affection. The novel deeply explores first impressions, class division, the financial vulnerability of women, and individual morality versus societal decorum. Ultimately, it delivers the message that true love and personal growth require individuals to conquer their own flaws of ego ("pride") and hasty judgment ("prejudice") before they can truly understand others. The main characters include Elizabeth Bennet, a sharp-tongued and perceptive heroine; Mr. Darcy, a wealthy, socially awkward nobleman with a hidden noble character; and Jane Bennet, Elizabeth’s gentle, optimistic eldest sister. The work is globally notable for being one of the most popular novels in English literature, celebrated for its timeless enemies-to-lovers trope, unforgettable cast, and masterclass execution of sharp irony.

Emma is a classic comic novel of manners written by Jane Austen and published in 1815. The masterpiece follows the "handsome, clever, and rich" aristocrat Emma Woodhouse as she busies herself playing matchmaker in her small country village with no apparent care for her own romantic life. After successfully pairing her governess, Emma stubbornly takes the naive Harriet Smith under her wing, sparking a series of misguided romantic schemes and painful social misunderstandings that constantly draw the criticism of her sharp-witted neighbor, Mr. George Knightley. The novel deeply explores youthful hubris, social class and status, the limits of good intentions, and gender limitations in Regency England. Ultimately, it delivers the message that true emotional maturity requires overcoming one's vanity and blind self-assurance before one can honestly understand the human heart. The main characters include Emma Woodhouse, the well-meaning but dangerously meddlesome heroine; Mr. George Knightley, her older, grounded family friend who acts as her moral compass; and Harriet Smith, the sweet but easily influenced young woman whom Emma repeatedly misguides. The work is globally notable for featuring Austen’s most fiercely independent and flawed protagonist—whom Austen famously predicted "no one but myself will much like"—and for its brilliant, early use of free indirect discourse to anchor the psychological realism of the narrative.

Nineteen Eighty-Four is a dystopian social science fiction novel written by George Orwell and published in 1949. Nineteen Eighty-Four follows Winston Smith as he struggles against an oppressive, totalitarian regime in the superstate of Oceania, where every aspect of human life is strictly controlled. Driven by a desire for truth and freedom, Winston commits "thoughtcrime" by starting a forbidden diary and engaging in a secret, illegal romance with a co-worker. However, his quiet rebellion is crushed when he is entrapped by the Inner Party, leading to severe psychological torture and his ultimate ideological submission.The novel explores totalitarianism, mass surveillance, psychological manipulation, and the erasure of objective truth. Orwell uses this bleak world to deliver a stark warning about how easily a government can strip away individual freedom and alter historical reality to maintain absolute power.The main characters include Winston Smith, Julia, and O'Brien. Winston is a low-ranking Party member who secretly hates the regime; Julia is his practical, rebellious lover who rejects the Party’s strict puritanical doctrines; and O'Brien is a powerful, manipulative official who poses as a resistance ally only to orchestrate Winston's brutal interrogation.The book is notable for coining widespread cultural terms—such as "Big Brother," "doublethink," and "Newspeak"—and for serving as the definitive literary benchmark for political warnings against government overreach, heavily influencing modern political discourse.

Animal Farm is an allegorical satirical novella written by George Orwell and published in 1945. Animal Farm follows a group of anthropomorphic farm animals as they rebel against their abusive human master, Mr. Jones, to create a society where all animals are equal and free. Over time, the utopian ideals of the rebellion are systematically corrupted by a power-hungry pig named Napoleon, who transforms the farm into a brutal dictatorship. The animals work harder and have less food than before, while the elite pig class adopts the worst traits of humans.The novel explores the corruption of socialist ideals, the rise of totalitarianism, class warfare, and the dangerous power of political propaganda. Orwell uses the fable to show how easily revolutionary movements can be subverted by individuals who value personal power over collective freedom.The main characters include Napoleon, Snowball, and Boxer. Napoleon is a fierce, manipulative pig who assumes leadership through military force and terror; Snowball is his idealistic and inventive rival who is exiled from the farm; and Boxer is a loyal, hard-working horse who embodies the exploited working class.The book is notable for its sharp political satire of the Russian Revolution of 1917 and the subsequent Stalinist era, as well as for introducing the iconic literary phrase: "All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others."

Burmese Days is a satirical, semi-autobiographical novel written by George Orwell and published in 1934. Burmese Days follows John Flory as a lonely British teak merchant struggling with the moral decay, racism, and stifling social climate of a remote colonial settlement in British-ruled Burma. Flory's miserable isolation temporarily lifts when he falls in love with Elizabeth Lackersteen, a newly arrived young Englishwoman whom he hopes can save him from his despondency. However, his dreams are systematically ruined by his own tragic weaknesses and the malicious machinations of a corrupt, manipulative native magistrate named U Po Kyin.The novel explores the evils of imperialism, intense loneliness and alienation, racial prejudice, and the corrupting nature of absolute institutional power. Orwell uses the narrative to expose how the British Empire degrades both the indigenous population and the psychological health of the colonizers themselves.The main characters include John Flory, Elizabeth Lackersteen, and Dr. Veraswami. Flory is a disillusioned white merchant with a facial birthmark that mirrors his inner shame; Elizabeth is a shallow, snobbish woman who intensely loathes the native culture; and Dr. Veraswami is a loyal Indian doctor who desperately seeks Flory's friendship to secure a prestigious, protective spot in the all-white European club.The book is notable for drawing directly from Orwell’s real-world experiences as a police officer in the Indian Imperial Police in Burma, serving as his earliest literary critique of political oppression before his later, more famous works

Keep the Aspidistra Flying is a socially critical, semi-autobiographical novel written by George Orwell and published in 1936. Keep the Aspidistra Flying follows Gordon Comstock as a cynical, idealistic young poet who declares war on the "money-god" by quitting his respectable advertising job to live a life of voluntary poverty. He takes a low-paying job at a bookstore, but his romantic view of poverty quickly dissolves into a miserable reality of squalor, hunger, and creative stagnation. His self-destructive rebellion pushes away his friends and threatens his relationship with his long-suffering girlfriend, Rosemary, until a sudden pregnancy forces him to re-evaluate his choices.The novel explores the omnipresent power of money, the realities of urban poverty, the death of artistic ambition, and the weight of social conformity. Orwell uses Gordon's descent to demonstrate how modern capitalist society makes true artistic independence impossible, forcing individuals to choose between financial submission and total destitution.The main characters include Gordon Comstock, Rosemary Waterlow, and Philip Ravelston. Gordon is the bitter, proud protagonist trapped in his own obsessive rebellion against wealth; Rosemary is his loyal, practical girlfriend who patiently endures his poverty-fueled mood swings; and Ravelston is Gordon's wealthy, Marxist friend who genuinely tries to help him but remains separated from Gordon's struggles by his own upper-class cushion.The book is notable for satirizing Orwell's own youthful anxieties regarding financial security and literary status, while using the titular houseplant—the aspidistra—as an enduring, ironic literary symbol for the dull, respectable British middle-class life that Gordon simultaneously despises and craves.

Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship is a Bildungsroman written by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and published in 1795–1796. Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship follows Wilhelm Meister, a young man who leaves his conventional life behind and becomes involved with a traveling theater company while searching for his purpose. His experiences with theater, friendship, love, failure, and different social classes gradually change his understanding of himself and his place in society. Over time, Wilhelm moves away from youthful idealism toward greater self-knowledge and responsibility. The novel explores self-discovery, education, personal growth, art, and society. The main characters include Wilhelm Meister, Mariane, and Jarno, who influence Wilhelm's emotional and intellectual development. The main characters include Eduard, Charlotte, Ottilie, and the Captain, whose relationships form the novel's central conflict. The book is notable for its use of scientific ideas as literary metaphors and for its sophisticated examination of marriage, desire, and social conventions.

Steppenwolf is a German classic written by Hermann Hesse and published in 1927. This philosophical and psychological novel follows Harry Haller, a lonely intellectual who struggles between his civilized identity and his wild, instinctive side. As Harry encounters new people and experiences, he begins questioning his isolated worldview and discovers that his personality is far more complex than the simple division between “man” and “wolf.” The novel explores identity, loneliness, alienation, individuality, and self-acceptance. Hesse suggests that human identity is complex and multifaceted, and that true growth comes from accepting the many sides of oneself. The main characters include Harry Haller, Hermine, and Pablo. Harry is the troubled protagonist, Hermine becomes his guide and companion, and Pablo introduces him to new experiences and perspectives. The book is notable for its experimental structure, psychological depth, and exploration of post-World War I European alienation.

The Glass Bead Game is a German classic written by Hermann Hesse and published in 1943. The novel explores alienation, identity, family, isolation, and dehumanization. The main characters include Gregor Samsa, the transformed protagonist; Grete Samsa, his sister who initially cares for him but eventually becomes distant; and Mr. Samsa, Gregor's father, who struggles to accept his son's transformation. The Trial is a modernist novel and psychological drama written by Franz Kafka and published posthumously in 1925.
